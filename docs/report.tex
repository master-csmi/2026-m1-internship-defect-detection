\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}


\subsection{The electrocardiogram and cardiac arrhythmias} \label{subsec:ecg}

Every heartbeat produces a small electrical signal that travels through the body and can be
recorded at the skin's surface. The resulting trace, the electrocardiogram (ECG), is one of
the oldest and most widely used diagnostic tools in medicine. In a healthy heart the signal
repeats itself beat after beat with the same characteristic shape: a P wave, a sharp QRS
complex and a T wave, separated by intervals whose duration is itself clinically
meaningful~\cite{taskforce1996hrv}. When something goes wrong, the shape of the beat, its
timing, or both, change. These irregularities are called arrhythmias, and some of them are
early warnings of serious cardiac conditions~\cite{luz2016survey}.

The difficulty is that arrhythmias are rare. At a resting rate of \SI{70}{\per\minute} a
patient produces more than one hundred thousand beats over twenty-four hours of monitoring,
of which only a small fraction are abnormal. The reference database used in this work, the
MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database~\cite{moody2001mitbih}, was assembled precisely to capture this
regime: it contains roughly \num{110000} expert-annotated beats, and even though the records
were deliberately selected to include clinically significant arrhythmias, normal beats still
dominate by a wide margin. Reviewing such a recording manually is slow and tiring, precisely
because the interesting events are buried in a vast amount of unremarkable data. The natural
response is to automate the search: to build a system that learns what a normal beat looks
like and flags anything that departs from it.




\subsection{The same problem in industry} \label{subsec:vibration}

This problem is not unique to medicine. In industry, rotating machines produce vibration
signals that behave in the same way. A machine in good condition vibrates with a stable,
repetitive pattern governed by its rotation speed and its geometry. As a fault develops,
whether through misalignment, imbalance or a growing crack in a bearing race, that pattern
changes before the machine actually fails, and the change is visible in the statistics and
in the spectrum of the vibration signal long before it is audible to an
operator~\cite{randall2011bearing}. Detecting these early changes is the foundation of
predictive maintenance, which aims to intervene before a breakdown rather than after
it~\cite{lei2018review}.

Despite coming from different worlds, the two situations share the same structure. In both
we observe a one-dimensional signal that evolves over time; in both the signal is mostly
normal and occasionally not; and in both the abnormal segments are rare, diverse, and costly
to label. In both, the central question is the same: how can a system automatically recognise
what departs from normal behaviour, without being told in advance what every possible
abnormality looks like?

\subsection{Why anomaly detection rather than classification} \label{subsec:why-anomaly-detection}

This last point is what makes the problem interesting rather than routine. Anomalies are not
simply a second class to be recognised alongside the first. They are rare, they are varied,
and new ones can always appear. A classifier trained to separate two balanced classes assumes
that both are well represented in the training data and that the test data will be drawn from
the same distribution; neither assumption holds here. Anomaly detection instead builds a model
of normality from data that is overwhelmingly normal, and treats a significant deviation from
that model as suspicious~\cite{chandola2009survey}. The literature distinguishes several
regimes along this axis, from fully supervised classification to purely unsupervised novelty
detection, and the choice between them is driven by how many labels are available and how much
one is willing to assume about the anomalies~\cite{ruff2021unifying}.

The MIT-BIH database is unusual in that it is fully annotated, which means both regimes can be
studied on exactly the same data. That is the opportunity this work exploits: rather than
choosing one approach, we implement methods from the whole spectrum (statistical control
charts, unsupervised detectors, and supervised classifiers) and evaluate them under a single
protocol. The labels are then used in two different roles: withheld, to simulate the realistic
situation where none exist, and used, to measure exactly what supervision buys.


\subsection{Objectives and outline of the report} \label{subsec:objectives}

The aim of this internship is to develop, implement and evaluate methods for detecting
anomalies in time-series signals, using the ECG as the main case study and treating vibration
signals as an extension of the same framework. The work relies on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia
Database, distributed by PhysioNet~\cite{goldberger2000physionet}. Four objectives structure
the work:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(O\arabic*)]
  \item establish a rigorous and reproducible evaluation protocol, based on the AAMI EC57
        beat groupings~\cite{aami2012ec57} and on the inter-patient train/test split
        introduced by de Chazal et al.~\cite{dechazal2004}, so that no patient contributes to
        both training and testing and the results remain comparable with published work;
  \item implement a graded sequence of detection methods, from simple statistical baselines
        through unsupervised models to supervised classifiers, all measured with the same
        metrics on the same test set;
  \item quantify what the addition of labels actually contributes, and identify which classes
        of arrhythmia remain difficult and why;
  \item deliver the whole study as reproducible software (an installable package, a test
        suite, continuous integration and a container image) so that every number in this
        report can be regenerated.
\end{enumerate}

The report follows the structure recommended by the CSMI internship
guide~\cite{csmiguide}. Section~\ref{sec:missions} states the missions and the measurable
objectives, and Section~\ref{sec:context} describes the scientific and technical context.
Section~\ref{sec:data} documents the dataset: its origin, its licence, its statistics, the
split, and its limitations. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} fixes the evaluation protocol and
justifies the choice of metrics under strong class imbalance. The methods are then presented
in increasing order of sophistication: statistical baselines in Section~\ref{sec:baselines},
feature engineering and unsupervised detection in Section~\ref{sec:unsupervised}, and
supervised classification in Section~\ref{sec:supervised}. Section~\ref{sec:software}
describes the software contributions. Sections~\ref{sec:verification}
to~\ref{sec:uq} address verification, validation and uncertainty quantification in turn.
Section~\ref{sec:results} compares all methods and discusses the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with the limitations of the work and the directions it
opens.
