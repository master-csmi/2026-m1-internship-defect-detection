\begin{center}
  {\large\bfseries Abstract}
\end{center}

\begin{quote}
\small
A long ECG recording contains more than a hundred thousand heartbeats, of which
only a small fraction are abnormal, and the same imbalance appears in the
vibration signals used for predictive maintenance in industry. This internship
asks how well a machine can find those rare events on its own, and what it costs
to help it with labels. Working on the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia
Database~\cite{moody2001mitbih}, a complete chain was built from raw signal to a
decision on every single beat, and ten methods were compared on it: three
statistical baselines, two label-free models on 35 engineered features, two
supervised classifiers and two autoencoders. All of them are measured under one
protocol, fixed before any model was written: records are split by patient
following de Chazal et al.~\cite{dechazal2004}, and the decision threshold is
chosen only on the training half.

Three results come out of the comparison. Supervision is worth about five times
what all the feature engineering is worth put together (F1 rising from
\num{0.427} to \num{0.721} on the same features), but what it buys is precision,
not coverage: the labels teach the model to stop flagging beats that are merely
unusual. Cost and accuracy do not move together, a forest of random cuts fitted
in seconds coming within \num{0.032} of a neural network trained for several
minutes. And the per-class results reverse the overall ranking on the hardest
class: supraventricular beats are caught four times better by an autoencoder that
never saw a label than by the best supervised model, because a model minimising
overall error under a nine-to-one imbalance learns that ignoring a rare class
costs it almost nothing. The two families therefore fail on different beats,
which points to combining them. The whole study is delivered as an installable
Python package with 64 tests, continuous integration and a published container
image, so that every number reported here can be produced again.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} anomaly detection, time series, ECG, MIT-BIH,
class imbalance, autoencoders, reproducible research.
\end{quote}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}


\subsection{The electrocardiogram and cardiac arrhythmias} \label{subsec:ecg}

Every heartbeat produces a small electrical signal that travels through the body and can be
recorded at the skin's surface. The resulting trace, the electrocardiogram (ECG), is one of
the oldest and most widely used diagnostic tools in medicine. In a healthy heart the signal
repeats itself beat after beat with the same characteristic shape: a P wave, a sharp QRS
complex and a T wave, separated by intervals whose duration is itself clinically
meaningful~\cite{taskforce1996hrv}. When something goes wrong, the shape of the beat, its
timing, or both, change. These irregularities are called arrhythmias, and some of them are
early warnings of serious cardiac conditions~\cite{luz2016survey}.

The difficulty is that arrhythmias are rare. At a resting rate of \SI{70}{\per\minute} a
patient produces more than one hundred thousand beats over twenty-four hours of monitoring,
of which only a small fraction are abnormal. The reference database used in this work, the
MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database~\cite{moody2001mitbih}, was assembled precisely to capture this
regime: it contains roughly \num{110000} expert-annotated beats, and even though the records
were deliberately selected to include clinically significant arrhythmias, normal beats still
dominate by a wide margin. Reviewing such a recording manually is slow and tiring, precisely
because the interesting events are buried in a vast amount of unremarkable data. The natural
response is to automate the search: to build a system that learns what a normal beat looks
like and flags anything that departs from it.




\subsection{The same problem in industry} \label{subsec:vibration}

This problem is not unique to medicine. In industry, rotating machines produce vibration
signals that behave in the same way. A machine in good condition vibrates with a stable,
repetitive pattern governed by its rotation speed and its geometry. As a fault develops,
whether through misalignment, imbalance or a growing crack in a bearing race, that pattern
changes before the machine actually fails, and the change is visible in the statistics and
in the spectrum of the vibration signal long before it is audible to an
operator~\cite{randall2011bearing}. Detecting these early changes is the foundation of
predictive maintenance, which aims to intervene before a breakdown rather than after
it~\cite{lei2018review}.

Despite coming from different worlds, the two situations share the same structure. In both
we observe a one-dimensional signal that evolves over time; in both the signal is mostly
normal and occasionally not; and in both the abnormal segments are rare, diverse, and costly
to label. In both, the central question is the same: how can a system automatically recognise
what departs from normal behaviour, without being told in advance what every possible
abnormality looks like?

\subsection{Why anomaly detection rather than classification} \label{subsec:why-anomaly-detection}

This last point is what makes the problem interesting rather than routine. Anomalies are not
simply a second class to be recognised alongside the first. They are rare, they are varied,
and new ones can always appear. A classifier trained to separate two balanced classes assumes
that both are well represented in the training data and that the test data will be drawn from
the same distribution; neither assumption holds here. Anomaly detection instead builds a model
of normality from data that is overwhelmingly normal, and treats a significant deviation from
that model as suspicious~\cite{chandola2009survey}. The literature distinguishes several
regimes along this axis, from fully supervised classification to purely unsupervised novelty
detection, and the choice between them is driven by how many labels are available and how much
one is willing to assume about the anomalies~\cite{ruff2021unifying}.

The MIT-BIH database is unusual in that it is fully annotated, which means both regimes can be
studied on exactly the same data. That is the opportunity this work exploits: rather than
choosing one approach, we implement methods from the whole spectrum (statistical control
charts, unsupervised detectors, and supervised classifiers) and evaluate them under a single
protocol. The labels are then used in two different roles: withheld, to simulate the realistic
situation where none exist, and used, to measure exactly what supervision buys.


\subsection{Objectives and outline of the report} \label{subsec:objectives}

The aim of this internship is to develop, implement and evaluate methods for detecting
anomalies in time-series signals, with the ECG as the case study. The work relies entirely on
the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, distributed by PhysioNet~\cite{goldberger2000physionet}: no
vibration data was used, and the industrial case of Section~\ref{subsec:vibration} serves as
motivation rather than as a second experiment. What the pipeline would need in order to be
carried over to a vibration signal is set out in Section~\ref{subsec:perspectives}. Four
objectives structure the work:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(O\arabic*)]
  \item establish a rigorous and reproducible evaluation protocol, based on the AAMI EC57
        beat groupings~\cite{aami2012ec57} and on the inter-patient train/test split
        introduced by de Chazal et al.~\cite{dechazal2004}, so that no patient contributes to
        both training and testing and the results remain comparable with published work;
  \item implement a graded sequence of detection methods, from simple statistical baselines
        through unsupervised models to supervised classifiers, all measured with the same
        metrics on the same test set;
  \item quantify what the addition of labels actually contributes, and identify which classes
        of arrhythmia remain difficult and why;
  \item deliver the whole study as reproducible software (an installable package, a test
        suite, continuous integration and a container image) so that every number in this
        report can be regenerated.
\end{enumerate}

The report follows the structure recommended by the CSMI internship
guide~\cite{csmiguide}. 



\section{Mission and Objectives} \label{sec:missions}

\subsection{Assigned missions} \label{subsec:missions}

The internship was defined around one question: can a machine find abnormal heartbeats
in a long ECG recording on its own? Three missions were assigned.

The first mission was to build a working chain from raw signal to decision. That 
meant reading the recordings, cleaning them, cutting them into single beats,
turning each beat into numbers, scoring each beat and finally saying normal or abnormal.
Every stage had to run end to end without manual steps.

The second mission was to cover the full range of methods rather than pick one.
Simple statistical rules, methods that never see a label and methods that learn from 
labels were required so that the report can say that each level of effort was worth it.
The comparison only makes sense if all of them are judged on the same test set with the
same numbers, so a fixed measuring protocol had to come before the models.

The third mission was to leave behind software someone else can run. A published 
report is not enough on its own: the code had to be installable as a package, covered
by tests checked automatically at every change and shipped as a container so that the 
results survive the internship~\cite{wilson2017goodpractices, sandve2013reproducible}.

\subsection{Constraints (data, schedule, tooling)} \label{subsec:constraints}

The dataset was decided from the start: the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia
Database~\cite{moody2001mitbih}, kept online by PhysioNet~\cite{goldberger2000physionet}.
It was not picked because it was easy to get, but because every single
heartbeat in it carries a label. That is what lets the same data be used
twice: once with the labels and once without them. The five beat groups
come from the AAMI EC57 standard~\cite{aami2012ec57}, so the results can be
described in the same words as other published work. The recordings weigh
about \SI{650}{\mega\byte}, which is far too much for a Git repository.
They are therefore downloaded only when needed: never committed, never
copied into the container image, and attached at run time instead.

All work was done on a laptop CPU and on free GitHub runners with two cores. 
No GPU was available at any point. This shaped several choices: the deep models 
are small, the grid searches are short, and the CPU-only build of PyTorch is 
installed on purpose, because the default one pulls in about \SI{2.5}{\giga\byte} 
of GPU libraries that would never be used.

The internship was organised in stages, each ending with a merged pull request, 
so that progress was visible and nothing was left half finished.

\section{Context} \label{sec:context}


\subsection{Host structure and scientific environment}\label{subsec:host}

The internship took place within the CSMI programme of the University of
Strasbourg, whose teaching is carried out jointly with the "Institut de Recherche
Mathématique Avancée" (IRMA) and the Cemosis platform. Cemosis exists to connect
mathematics with other fields and with companies, so the projects it hosts are
rarely purely theoretical: they usually start from a real signal, a real
measurement or a real industrial need, and end with software that someone else
has to be able to run. The programme itself is built around scientific computing,
machine learning and simulation, so a topic mixing signal processing, statistics
and software engineering sits naturally inside it~\cite{csmiguide}.

This background explains two features of work that would look like extra effort. 
The first is the weight given to the software that means the container, the tests
and the automatic checks are treated here as results. The second is the way the 
problem is posed because a heartbeat and the vibration have nothing in 
common medically, but they lead to the same mathematical question, and the habit 
of looking for that shared structure comes from the programme's applied-mathematics 
culture rather than from cardiology.



\subsection{Existing tools, datasets and imposed choices}\label{subsec:existing}

The internship started from an empty repository: the package layout, the
naming convention and the continuous integration workflow were all built as
part of the work. Three choices were settled before any model was
written and never reopened.

The dataset is the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database~\cite{moody2001mitbih}. It
carries a cardiologist's label on every single beat, which is what allows
the same recordings to be used twice: once pretending the labels do not
exist, once using them. Beats are described with the five groups of the
AAMI EC57 standard (N, S, V, F, Q)~\cite{aami2012ec57} rather than the raw
annotation symbols, since any other vocabulary would be impossible to
compare with the literature. Finally, the data is split by patient
following de Chazal et al.~\cite{dechazal2004}: beats from one patient
resemble each other far more than beats from different patients, so a split
that lets the same patient appear on both sides measures memory rather than
generalisation. This is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:data}; what matters
here is that it was fixed before any model existed, so no result could be
tuned against the test set.



\subsection{State of the art in ECG beat classification}\label{subsec:sota}

Finding where the beats are was solved in the 1980s: the detector of
Pan and Tompkins~\cite{pan1985qrs} filters the signal to keep the frequency
band of the QRS complex, squares it to make the peaks stand out, and reads
them from a sliding window. Variants are still in use today. Deciding what
kind of beat has been found is the part that is still open, and three
families of approach dominate.

The first two families are the reference in the literature, but both assume
a large annotated set. The third is the one this work is centred on: it
matches the situation of most real deployments, where nobody has annotated
a hundred thousand beats in advance.

Two lessons from the literature shaped this internship directly. The first
is methodological: the survey of Luz et al.~\cite{luz2016survey} shows that
many published scores were obtained with patients present on both sides of
the split, and are therefore much higher than the same method achieves
under the stricter protocol; any comparison must first state which protocol
it uses. The second is about where the difficulty lies. Ventricular beats
are wide and misshapen, so nearly every method detects them. Supraventricular
beats arrive early but keep an almost normal shape, so timing is the only
clue. They remain the weak point of published systems, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows they are the weak point of this one too.



\section{Data} \label{sec:data}
\subsection{Origin, licence and ethics}\label{subsec:origin}

The recordings come from the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database~\cite{moody2001mitbih},
distributed by PhysioNet~\cite{goldberger2000physionet}. They were collected at
the Beth Israel Hospital in Boston in the 1970s and released for open research
use, so no new patient was recorded for this work and no ethical approval was
needed. The data carry no name, no date of birth and no hospital identifier: a
patient is only a three-digit record number.

The files are not stored in the repository. A script downloads them on demand
into a local folder, and that folder is mounted inside the container at run time.
This keeps the repository small and avoids redistributing data that already has
an official home.

\subsection{Description: records, sampling, annotations, AAMI classes}\label{subsec:description}

The database holds 48 recordings of about half an hour each, sampled at
\SI{360}{\hertz} on two leads. Only the first lead, MLII, is used here, because it
is the one present in almost every record; keeping both would mean handling
records where the second lead changes from one patient to the next.

What makes this database usable for the present work is its annotation. A
cardiologist marked every single beat, giving both its position in the signal and
its type. The position is taken as ground truth, so no beat detector is needed and
no detection error can contaminate the results. The type is a letter among about
fifteen possible symbols, which are grouped into the five AAMI
classes~\cite{aami2012ec57} as shown in \Cref{tab:aami}.\\

There are about 113 normal beats for every fusion beat. This single fact drives
most of the choices made later: a model that answers ``normal'' every time is
right more than four times out of five, so plain accuracy is useless here, and the
rare classes need care during training. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} returns to
this.

Four records (102, 104, 107 and 217) come from patients fitted with a pacemaker.
Their beats are shaped by the device rather than by the heart, so they follow
rules no model can learn from the others. They are excluded, as is standard
practice~\cite{aami2012ec57, dechazal2004}. This also explains why the Q class
nearly disappears from the working set: most of its beats belong to those four
records.

\subsection{Inter-patient split DS1/DS2 and leakage control}\label{subsec:split}

The 44 remaining records are cut into two groups of 22, following de Chazal et
al.~\cite{dechazal2004}: DS1 for training, DS2 for testing. The cut is made at the
level of the record, so a patient is either entirely in training or entirely in
testing, never in both.

This matters more than it may seem. Two beats from the same patient look almost
identical, so if the same patient appears on both sides, a model can score very
well simply by recognising that patient. The number obtained then says nothing
about a new patient walking into a clinic. Published scores obtained without this
precaution are much higher than the ones in this report, and they are not
comparable~\cite{luz2016survey}.

The split is not a comment in a notebook but a fixed list in the code, guarded by
checks that run in the test suite: no record can be in both sets, no paced record
can slip back in, and each set must contain exactly 22 records. A leak becomes a
failing test rather than a silent mistake.

\subsection{Pre-processing: filtering, beat segmentation, normalisation} \label{subsec:preprocessing}

Three steps turn a raw half-hour recording into beats a model can read.

\paragraph{Filtering.} A band-pass filter keeps only the frequencies between
\SI{0.5}{\hertz} and \SI{10}{\hertz}. Below that lies baseline wander, the slow
drift caused by breathing and by electrodes moving; above it lies mains hum and
muscle noise. The filter is applied forwards and then backwards, which cancels the
delay it would otherwise introduce. This was checked directly: a single spike fed
through the filter comes out at exactly the same sample, so the R-peak positions
given by the annotations stay valid.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{statistical_baseline/figures/raw_vs_filtered.png}
  \caption{Ten seconds of record 105 before and after filtering. The slow drift
  and the fuzz are gone while the peaks keep their position and their relative
  height. The effect is not cosmetic: at a fixed threshold of 3, the number of
  flagged samples changes by about \SI{19}{\percent} between the two signals.}
  \label{fig:rawfiltered}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Segmentation.} Around each annotated R-peak a window is cut, from
\SI{0.25}{\second} before to \SI{0.40}{\second} after, that is 234 samples. The
window is not symmetric on purpose: the P wave sits close before the peak while
the T wave stretches further after it. Beats too close to the start or the end of
a recording are dropped, since their window would fall outside the signal.

\paragraph{Normalisation.} Each recording is centred and divided by its own
standard deviation. Electrode placement and skin contact change the amplitude from
one patient to the next, and without this step a model would learn the recording
conditions instead of the shape of the beat.


\subsection{Relevance and limitations}\label{subsec:datalimits}

This database is the right choice for the question asked, but it is not a fair
picture of clinical reality, and three points must be stated plainly.

It is small and old. Forty-eight recordings from the 1970s, on equipment long
replaced, with a patient group chosen by the original authors to contain many
arrhythmias. Real hospital data would be noisier and far more lopsided.

The labels are treated as truth, yet they were placed by humans. Cardiologists
disagree on borderline beats, so part of what a model is asked to reproduce is
one team's judgement rather than an absolute fact.

The beats are given, not found. Because the annotations supply the R-peak
positions, this work never faces the errors a real system would make while looking
for the beats in the first place. Every score in this report is therefore an
optimistic view of what a complete pipeline would achieve.





\section{Evaluation Protocol} \label{sec:evaluation}

Every method in this report is judged by the same rules, fixed once and written in
a single module reused everywhere. This section states those rules before any
model appears, so that no choice made later can be adjusted to flatter a result.

\subsection{Beat-level formulation of the detection problem}\label{subsec:formulation}

The unit of decision is the beat, not the recording and not a window of signal. A
recording is a list of beats, and each beat gets its own answer.

The five AAMI classes are then folded into two. A beat labelled N is normal; a
beat labelled S, V, F or Q is an anomaly. This turns the task into a yes-or-no
question, and there are three reasons for it.

The first is that it matches the goal. The aim is to find beats worth a second
look, not to name the exact arrhythmia, which is a physician's job.

The second is that it puts every method on the same footing. Isolation Forest and
an autoencoder do not produce a class, they produce a single number saying how
strange a beat looks. A five-class task would leave them out of the comparison
entirely.

The third is that the rarest classes are too thin to support a five-way decision
under a strict split. Fusion beats are well under one percent of the data, and a
score computed on so few examples moves far too much to mean anything.

Each method therefore outputs one number per beat, high meaning suspicious. A
threshold turns that number into a decision. The class labels are not thrown away:
they come back in Section~\ref{sec:results} to show which kinds of beat each
method actually finds.


\subsection{Metrics under strong imbalance}\label{subsec:metrics}

About one beat in six is an anomaly, so accuracy is discarded from the start: a
model that always answers ``normal'' would score above \SI{80}{\percent} while
finding nothing. Four numbers are reported instead, all computed on DS2, and
\Cref{tab:metrics} says what each one is for.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Reported metrics and what each one answers.}
  \label{tab:metrics}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.30\textwidth}p{0.34\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Metric} & \textbf{Question it answers} & \textbf{Why it is needed} \\
    \midrule
    Precision & Of the beats flagged, how many really were abnormal?
      & A low value means a physician wastes time on false alarms \\
    \addlinespace
    Recall & Of the abnormal beats, how many were flagged?
      & A low value means sick beats are missed, which is the costlier error \\
    \addlinespace
    F1 & The balance between the two above
      & A single number to rank methods, since one can always be raised by
        sacrificing the other \\
    \addlinespace
    ROC AUC & How well the scores separate the two groups, at any threshold
      & Judges the score itself, independently of where the cut is placed \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}


F1 and ROC AUC answer different questions and can disagree, which is informative
rather than awkward: a method with a good AUC and a poor F1 ranks beats correctly
but has been cut in the wrong place.

The four raw counts behind these numbers, true and false positives and negatives,
are stored alongside them for every experiment. They allow any figure in this
report to be recomputed, and they are what makes the error analysis of
Section~\ref{sec:validation} possible.


\subsection{Threshold selection on DS1}\label{subsec:threshold}

A score becomes a decision only once a threshold is chosen, and the choice changes
the result completely. Picking it on the test set would mean reporting the best
possible outcome rather than an honest one.

The threshold is therefore always chosen on DS1, the training half. Two hundred
candidate values are taken across the range of the training scores, each is tried,
and the one giving the best F1 on DS1 is kept. That value is then frozen and
applied to DS2 without any further adjustment.

The consequence is deliberate: the threshold is slightly wrong for DS2, because
patients differ and the score distribution shifts between the two halves. The
reported numbers are a little lower than the best achievable, and that gap is
exactly what a real system would face on a new patient.

\subsection{Reference baselines}\label{subsec:baselines}

A score means nothing on its own, so three reference points frame every result.

The lowest is chance: a detector flagging beats at random, whose precision equals
the share of anomalies in the data. Anything not clearly above it has learned
nothing.

The middle one is the statistical baselines of Section~\ref{sec:statistical},
which use no machine learning at all. A method costing far more computing power
than these has to justify the cost by beating them.

The highest is the supervised models of Section~\ref{sec:supervised}, which are
trained on the labels. They mark what the same features are worth when the answers
are known, and the distance between them and the label-free methods is the central
measurement of this work.

Published results are used as a fourth reference, but only those obtained under
the same inter-patient split. Comparing against numbers from the easier protocol
would be meaningless, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} handles that comparison
carefully.




\section{Statistical Baselines} \label{sec:statistical}

Before any learning, three simple detectors were built. None of them is trained
and none uses a label. They exist to set the floor: a method that costs a
thousand times more computing power has to beat these to be worth anything.

\subsection{Z-score on sliding windows} \label{subsec:zscore}

The idea is the oldest one in statistics. Around each sample, a window of the
signal is taken, its average and its spread are computed, and the sample is scored
by how many spreads it sits away from that average. The absolute value is used, so
a spike upwards and a dip downwards count the same.

Everything depends on the width of the window, which is what ``normal'' is
measured against. Two widths were tried, one second and ten seconds, and they
behave in opposite ways.

The one-second window fails completely. It is barely wider than a beat, so the
window sits mostly inside the beat it is judging: the beat becomes its own
reference, and the score no longer separates anything. Its ROC AUC is
\num{0.274}, below the \num{0.5} of pure chance. That is not a small failure, it
is an inversion: the detector ranks abnormal beats as \emph{more} normal than the
rest. \Cref{fig:zscore1s} shows why.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{statistical_baseline/figures/zscore_one_second.png}
  \caption{Z-score with a one-second window on record 105. Every beat crosses the
  line, normal ones included, because the window is barely wider than a beat and
  so contains almost nothing to compare against. The detector marks the R-peaks
  rather than the abnormal beats.}
  \label{fig:zscore1s}
\end{figure}


The ten-second window works, in a narrow sense. Covering roughly ten beats, it
gives a real reference (\Cref{fig:zscore10s}), and its precision reaches
\num{0.652}: when it flags a beat, it is usually right. But its recall is
\num{0.175}, so it misses more than four abnormal beats out of five. It only
catches the tall and obvious ones.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{statistical_baseline/figures/zscore_ten_seconds.png}
  \caption{The same signal scored with a ten-second window. The score no longer
  rises at every beat, because the window now holds about ten beats to compare
  against. Only the beats that stand out from their neighbours cross the line.}
  \label{fig:zscore10s}
\end{figure}

\subsection{CUSUM} \label{subsec:cusum}

The Z-score looks at each sample alone, so a small but lasting change goes
unnoticed. CUSUM is built for exactly that case. It keeps a running total: at each
step it adds how far the signal is from normal, minus a small allowance $k$, and
resets the total to zero whenever it would go negative. Small deviations that keep
pointing the same way accumulate; random noise cancels itself out. Two totals are
kept, one for drift upwards and one for drift downwards, and the score is the
larger.

The allowance $k$ decides what counts as too small to bother with. Four values
were tried; $k = 0.5$ was kept. A smaller $k$ makes the total climb on noise
alone, a larger one lets real drift pass unnoticed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{statistical_baseline/figures/cusum_score_k_0.5.png}
  \caption{CUSUM score with $k=0.5$ on record 105. The total climbs at each beat
  and falls back to zero between beats, which shows the accumulation working.
  It also shows the method's limit: the score stays high well after the peak
  that caused it, so it marks a stretch of signal rather than one beat.}
  \label{fig:cusum}
\end{figure}

CUSUM reaches an F1 of \num{0.369} with a ROC AUC of \num{0.680}, better balanced
than the Z-score but still far from usable. Its weakness is inherent to the
method: a total that accumulates keeps rising after the event that caused it, so
the score marks a stretch of signal rather than a single beat.

\subsection{Spectral features} \label{subsec:spectral}

The first two detectors read the signal in time. The third reads it in frequency,
which is a better match for the problem, since an abnormal beat is often abnormal
in shape: a ventricular beat is wide and smooth, so its energy sits lower in the
spectrum than a normal beat's sharp spike.

Each beat is transformed with a Fourier transform, after multiplying it by a Hann
window so that the cut edges of the beat do not add energy that is not really
there. Six numbers are then read off the spectrum (\Cref{tab:spectralfeat}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{The six spectral descriptors computed per beat.}
  \label{tab:spectralfeat}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.52\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Descriptor} & \textbf{What it measures} \\
    \midrule
    Centroid   & Where the energy sits on average: the ``pitch'' of the beat \\
    Bandwidth  & How widely the energy is spread around that centre \\
    Entropy    & Whether the energy is concentrated or scattered everywhere \\
    Dominant frequency & The single strongest frequency \\
    Low-frequency ratio  & Share of the energy below \SI{10}{\hertz} \\
    High-frequency ratio & Share of the energy above \SI{20}{\hertz} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Each of the six is turned into a Z-score across all beats, and the score of a beat
is the largest of the six. A beat is therefore flagged if it is unusual in any one
respect, rather than having to be unusual in all of them.

This is the best of the three baselines: F1 \num{0.373} and ROC AUC \num{0.773}.
The split between the two numbers is worth noting, since it recurs for every method
that follows: the ranking is decent while the threshold sits badly, so the score
carries more information than the yes-or-no decision manages to use.

\Cref{tab:statbaselines} collects the results on DS2, with a random detector added
for reference.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Statistical baselines on the test set DS2. Best F1 in bold.}
  \label{tab:statbaselines}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{Precision} & \textbf{Recall} & \textbf{F1}
      & \textbf{ROC AUC} \\
    \midrule
    Spectral features    & 0.297 & 0.502 & \textbf{0.373} & 0.773 \\
    CUSUM ($k=0.5$)      & 0.410 & 0.335 & 0.369 & 0.680 \\

Four points deserve to be kept.

The random detector shows why precision must be read next to recall. It flags
almost everything, so its recall is \num{0.996}, and its precision of \num{0.110}
is simply the share of abnormal beats in DS2. Any method whose precision is near
\num{0.11} has learned nothing, whatever its recall.

The one-second Z-score is worse than random, and this is the most useful failure
of the section. It shows that a badly chosen scale does not merely weaken a
detector, it can reverse it. The window has to be wide enough to contain a
reference the beat is not part of.

Every baseline trades precision against recall and none escapes the trade. The
ten-second Z-score is precise but nearly blind; the spectral detector sees twice
as much but is wrong twice as often when it speaks. No threshold fixes this,
because the underlying scores are simply not sharp enough.

Finally, the best of the three reaches an F1 of \num{0.373}. That is the number
every later method has to beat, and it was obtained without a single label and in
a few seconds of computation.

The lesson carried into the next section is where the limit lies. Each of these
detectors reduces a beat to one number, chosen in advance. A beat is not one
number: it has a duration, a shape, a timing relative to its neighbours. The next
step is to describe a beat with a proper set of measurements and let a model
combine them.



\section{Feature Engineering and Unsupervised Detection} \label{sec:features}

The baselines of Section~\ref{sec:statistical} all failed the same way: each one
squeezed a beat into a single number decided in advance. This section does the
opposite. Every beat is described by 35 numbers from four different points of
view, and a model is left to work out which combinations are unusual. The labels
are still not used for training.

\subsection{Rhythm features} \label{subsec:rhythm}

Rhythm is about \emph{when} a beat arrives, not what it looks like. The building
block is the RR interval, the time between one R-peak and the next. Nine numbers
are computed per beat (\Cref{tab:rhythmfeat}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{The nine rhythm features.}
  \label{tab:rhythmfeat}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.55\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Feature} & \textbf{What it measures} \\
    \midrule
    \texttt{pre\_rr}, \texttt{post\_rr} & Time to the previous and to the next beat \\
    \texttt{rr\_ratio}      & Whether the beat sits early or late between its neighbours \\
    \texttt{local\_rr\_mean}& The patient's current pace, over the ten nearest beats \\
    \texttt{rr\_deviation}  & This beat's gap compared with that local pace \\
    \texttt{local\_sdnn}    & How irregular the rhythm is around this beat \\
    \texttt{local\_rmssd}   & How much the gap jumps from one beat to the next \\
    \texttt{local\_pnn50}   & Share of nearby gaps changing by more than \SI{50}{\milli\second} \\
    \texttt{heart\_rate}    & Beats per minute at this point \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The word \emph{local} carries the whole idea. A resting athlete and a patient with
a fast heart have very different gaps, so an absolute value cannot say much. What
matters is whether a beat breaks \emph{that patient's} current pattern, so every
comparison is made against a window of ten surrounding beats rather than against
the whole recording.

This is also the only family of features able to see a supraventricular beat. An S
beat keeps a nearly normal shape and betrays itself only by arriving too early,
which shows up in \texttt{rr\_deviation} and nowhere else.

Two edge cases had to be handled: the first beat of a record has nothing before it
and the last has nothing after it. Both are filled with the median gap of the
record, which avoids inventing an extreme value at either end.

\subsection{Morphological features} \label{subsec:morphological}

Morphology is about the shape of the beat window itself, and ten numbers describe
it: the root mean square, the variance, the standard deviation, the peak-to-peak
range, the largest absolute value, the total energy, the mean absolute deviation,
the skewness, the kurtosis, and the number of zero crossings.

Most of these say how big the beat is and how spread out its values are. Three are
worth singling out. Skewness says whether the beat leans up or down, which
separates a sharp positive spike from the deep dip that follows a ventricular
beat. Kurtosis says whether the energy is concentrated in one peak or spread
across the window: a normal beat is a narrow spike and scores high, a wide V beat
scores much lower. Zero crossings count how often the signal changes sign, which
rises sharply on a noisy or fragmented beat.

Two of them are undefined on a perfectly flat window, since both divide by the
spread. They are set to zero in that case rather than allowed to become infinite,
which would break every model downstream.

\subsection{Wavelet features} \label{subsec:wavelets}

The Fourier transform used in Section~\ref{subsec:spectral} says which frequencies
are present in a beat, but not \emph{when} inside the beat they occur. That
information matters here: a normal beat and a ventricular beat can hold similar
frequencies arranged in a completely different order. Wavelets keep both axes, and
two transforms are used.

The discrete transform (DWT, Daubechies-4, four levels) splits the beat into five
bands, from the slow overall shape down to the fastest detail. For each band, two
numbers are kept: the share of the beat's energy it holds, and how much that band
varies. Because the transform conserves energy, the five shares add up to one, so
they describe how the beat spreads its energy rather than how loud it is. A wide,
smooth abnormal beat pushes its energy into the slow bands; a sharp or noisy one
pushes it into the fast ones.

The continuous transform (CWT, Morlet wavelet) is what actually keeps the timing.
It produces a scalogram, a picture of the beat with time across and frequency up
(\Cref{fig:scalogram}). Four frequency bands are read off it, and each gives two
numbers: how much energy the band holds, and at which moment inside the beat that
energy peaks, expressed as a position between 0 at the start of the window and 1
at the end. That second number is the one the Fourier transform simply cannot
produce.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{features_unsupervised/figures/scalogram_normal_vs_v.png}
  \caption{A normal beat (left) and a ventricular beat (right), each above its
  scalogram. Bright means energy. The normal beat keeps its energy low and
  narrow, concentrated at the instant of the spike. The ventricular beat is far
  brighter and reaches much higher up the scale axis, and its energy is spread
  over a wider stretch of the window. The two axes together are what a plain
  Fourier transform would collapse into one.}
  \label{fig:scalogram}
\end{figure}


Put side by side, the four families give 35 numbers per beat: 9 rhythm, 10
morphological, 6 spectral and 10 wavelet. They are computed once for DS1 and once
for DS2 and saved as tables, so every model that follows reads the same numbers
and no result depends on recomputing them.

\subsection{Isolation Forest} \label{subsec:iforest}

Isolation Forest works from an idea that is almost too simple. Pick a feature at
random, pick a cut point at random, split the data, and repeat. A point in the
middle of the crowd needs many cuts before it ends up alone. A point far from the
others gets separated after a few. The average number of cuts needed to isolate a
beat becomes its score.

Two hundred such trees are grown. The \texttt{contamination} setting is left at
\num{0.1}, meaning the model assumes about one beat in ten is unusual, which is
close to the real share in DS2 without having been read from it. The sign of the
output is flipped so that, here as everywhere else in this report, a high score
means suspicious.

The features are standardised first. The trees themselves do not need it, since a
split on one feature ignores the scale of the others, but it keeps the preparation
identical to the one the SVM requires, so the two models are compared on exactly
the same input.

\subsection{One-class SVM} \label{subsec:ocsvm}

The one-class SVM takes the opposite route. Instead of separating points, it draws
a boundary around the region where the training data lives and flags whatever
falls outside. The boundary is drawn with an RBF kernel, so it can curve rather
than being a straight line. The setting $\nu = 0.1$ is roughly the share of
training beats the model is allowed to leave outside its own boundary.

Standardisation is not optional here. The RBF kernel measures distances between
beats, so a feature with large raw values, such as energy, would dominate every
distance and the others would be ignored.

One practical obstacle had to be handled. The cost of this model grows with the
square of the number of beats, and DS1 holds roughly 50\,000 of them, which makes
a full fit impractical on the available hardware. The model is therefore trained
on a random sample of 20\,000 beats, drawn with a fixed seed so the result can be
reproduced. Scoring is still done on every beat of DS2.

\Cref{tab:unsupresults} adds the two models to the baselines of the previous

Isolation Forest takes the lead on both numbers, and the AUC is the more

threshold. Thirty-five features combined by a model beat one number chosen by
hand, which is the result this section was built to test.

The One-Class SVM is the surprise, and not a good one. Despite being the more
sophisticated method it lands below the spectral baseline, with a precision of
\num{0.200} that is barely above chance. Three explanations are plausible and the
data does not separate them cleanly. It saw only 20\,000 beats rather than
50\,000. Its single boundary must enclose the normal beats of 22 different
patients at once, which is a much harder shape than a forest of independent cuts
has to fit. And its two settings, $\nu$ and $\gamma$, were left at sensible
defaults rather than searched, because searching them without touching DS2 was too
costly here. The honest conclusion is that the model is not ruled out, only that
it was not made to work under these constraints.

The gap between F1 and AUC persists for Isolation Forest: an AUC of \num{0.836}
against an F1 of \num{0.427}. Section~\ref{subsec:overall} returns to this
pattern, which every method in this report shares.

Overall the best label-free method reaches an F1 of \num{0.427}, against
\num{0.373} for the best method costing nothing. That is a real gain but a modest
one, and it comes at a much higher price in features and computation. Whether the
labels are worth more than the features is exactly the question
Section~\ref{sec:supervised} answers, using the same 35 numbers.



\section{Supervised Classification} \label{sec:supervised}

Everything so far worked without labels. This section uses them. The features are
exactly the same 35 numbers as in Section~\ref{sec:features}, and the split, the
metrics and the threshold rule are unchanged. The only difference is that the
model is now told, during training, which beats are abnormal. Whatever the scores
gain is therefore the price of the labels and nothing else.

\subsection{Random Forest} \label{subsec:rf}

A single decision tree learns fast and remembers too much. A forest grows two
hundred of them, each on a different random view of the data, and averages their
votes. What one tree learned by accident the others do not repeat, so the average
is far steadier than any of its parts.

Four settings were searched over a small grid: the maximum depth of a tree,
the smallest number of beats allowed in a leaf, and whether the rare class is
given extra weight. The grid was kept deliberately small, enough to see whether
tuning helps at all without spending a day of computation on it.

The search uses grouped cross-validation, and this is the point that matters most
in the whole section. An ordinary five-fold split would scatter one patient's
beats across all five folds, so the model would be validated on beats from a
patient it had already trained on, and the search would pick the settings best at
recognising patients. Grouping by record keeps every beat of a patient inside a
single fold. It is the same precaution as the DS1/DS2 split, applied one level
down, inside the training half.

\subsection{Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)} \label{subsec:xgb}

A forest builds its trees independently. Boosting builds them one after another,
each new tree working on what the previous ones got wrong. The result is usually
sharper, at the price of being easier to overfit, which is why the learning rate
matters: it decides how much of each new tree's correction is actually applied.

Four settings were searched: the number of trees, their depth, the learning rate,
and the weight given to the rare class. That last one is not a fixed number. It is
computed from the data as the ratio of normal to abnormal beats, so it adapts if
the imbalance changes rather than hard-coding a value that is only correct for
this dataset.

\subsection{Handling imbalance} \label{subsec:imbalance-handling}

About nine beats in ten are normal, so a model minimising its total error can do
very well by rarely saying anything else. Both models are given a way out of that
trap, and in both cases it is a weight rather than a change to the data.

The Random Forest can set \texttt{class\_weight} to balanced, which makes a
mistake on an abnormal beat count as much as many mistakes on normal ones. XGBoost
has \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight}, which does the same thing. In both cases the
weighted and unweighted versions were put in the grid, so the search decides
whether reweighting actually helps rather than it being assumed.

Two alternatives were rejected. Discarding normal beats to even out the counts
would throw away most of the data, including all the variety of normal beats
across 22 patients, which is precisely what the model needs to learn. Creating
artificial abnormal beats would invent examples nobody recorded, and on a medical
signal that is hard to defend.

\subsection{Interpretability with SHAP} \label{subsec:shap}

A forest of two hundred trees gives no readable rule, which is a serious problem
if a physician is ever meant to trust it. SHAP addresses this by measuring, for
one beat, how much each feature pushed the decision towards ``anomaly'' or away
from it~\cite{lundberg2017shap}. Averaged over many beats it says which features
matter in general (\Cref{fig:shapimp}); read on a single beat it explains that one
decision (\Cref{fig:shapone}).

The explanation is computed on a sample of one thousand beats. Explaining all
fifty thousand would take far longer and the picture does not change.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{supervised_ml/figures/shap_importance.png}
  \caption{The fifteen features that weigh most in the Random Forest, averaged
  over a sample of beats. The four at the top are all rhythm features, and they
  are well clear of the rest. Shape and frequency features appear next, but each
  contributes much less on its own.}
  \label{fig:shapimp}
\end{figure}


The ranking is worth reading closely, because it was not designed. Nobody told the
model that rhythm matters more than shape; it found that out from the data, and it
agrees with what a cardiologist would say. \texttt{heart\_rate} and
\texttt{rr\_deviation} lead, followed by \texttt{pre\_rr} and \texttt{rr\_ratio}:
all four are about \emph{when} the beat arrived. The strongest shape feature,
\texttt{spectral\_centroid}, comes only fifth, and the wavelet features sit near
the bottom of the list.

This is a useful and slightly uncomfortable result. Ten wavelet numbers per beat
cost most of the computation time of the feature step, and they contribute little
compared with a simple ratio of two intervals.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{supervised_ml/figures/shap_one_beat.png}
  \caption{The same analysis on one single beat, a ventricular beat from record
  200. Every bar points towards ``anomaly''. The decision rests first on the beat
  arriving out of step with its neighbours, then on its frequency content being
  shifted low. This is the kind of short, checkable account a physician could be
  shown alongside a flag.}
  \label{fig:shapone}
\end{figure}

it trained on, so a threshold picked from those scores would be badly wrong on new
data. Each DS1 beat is therefore scored by the folds that did \emph{not} train on

beats are the real ceiling: they keep a normal shape, so the only clue is timing,
and \Cref{fig:shapimp} shows the model leaning on exactly that. It is using the
one signal available and still missing them.




\section{Deep Learning: Autoencoders} \label{sec:deep}

The methods so far read numbers computed by hand. This section removes that step
and lets a neural network read the beat itself. The labels are put away again: as
in Section~\ref{sec:features}, nothing tells the model which beats are abnormal.

\subsection{Principle: reconstruction error as an anomaly score}
\label{subsec:aeprinciple}

An autoencoder is asked to give back exactly what it was given. On its own that is
a pointless task, since copying the input would solve it. The trick is the
bottleneck: between the input and the output, the whole window is squeezed into
sixteen numbers. Two hundred and thirty-four samples cannot pass through sixteen
numbers unchanged, so the model has no choice but to learn what a beat generally
looks like and rebuild it from that summary~\cite{hinton2006reducing}.

The model is then trained on normal beats only. It becomes good at rebuilding
normal beats and has never had reason to learn anything else. Feeding it an
abnormal beat, the reconstruction comes back wrong, and how wrong it is becomes
the anomaly score~\cite{sakurada2014autoencoder}. High error means the beat does
not look like anything the model learned.

The threshold follows the same logic, and needs no anomaly label at all. The error
is measured on normal beats from five records held out of training, and the
threshold is set at a high percentile of those errors. In other words: accept that
a small share of normal beats will be flagged, and call anything above that line
an anomaly. Holding out whole records rather than random beats is the same
precaution as the DS1/DS2 split, applied inside the training half.

One reference point was fixed before any training. The laziest possible model
answers the average training window whatever it is shown. Its error is computed
first, and any autoencoder that does not clearly beat it has learned nothing,
whatever its scores look like afterwards.

\subsection{Convolutional autoencoder} \label{subsec:cnnae}

The first model reads the short beat window, the same 234 samples used everywhere
else in this report. Three convolutions slide small filters along the signal, each
halving its length, so the model looks first at fine detail and then at the
overall shape. A linear layer squeezes the result into sixteen numbers, and the
decoder walks the same path backwards, stretching the signal out and smoothing it
with convolutions until the original length is recovered.

One choice is worth noting. The decoder stretches the signal and then filters it,
rather than using a transposed convolution. The two do the same job, but the
lengths are far easier to follow this way, and the length has to come out exactly
right at the end.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{autoencoders/figures/cnn_reconstructions.png}
  \caption{Four beats and what the trained model gives back. The normal beat is
  rebuilt almost perfectly. The supraventricular beat has the highest error of
  the four, but the reconstruction still follows it closely. The ventricular and
  fusion beats come back with errors as low as the normal one: their shapes are
  smooth and slow, which the model finds easy to rebuild even though it never
  learned them. This is the weakness the section ends on.}
  \label{fig:cnnrecon}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Recurrent autoencoder} \label{subsec:lstmae}

The convolutional model sees one beat and nothing around it, so it can never
notice a beat arriving too early. The second model is built to fix that. Its
window is two seconds wide, centred on the same R-peak, so the beat before and the
beat after are inside it. An LSTM then walks along the window step by step, keeping
a memory of what it has seen, which lets it use the distance between beats and not
only their shape.

The window is decimated by six before being read. Since the filter of
Section~\ref{subsec:preprocessing} keeps nothing above \SI{10}{\hertz}, keeping
one sample in six loses no real information and makes the sequence six times
shorter, which an LSTM badly needs.

The first version of this model did not work. It settled at a reconstruction error
of about \num{0.95}, which on a signal of variance one is what a flat line scores:
the model had given up and was answering nothing at all. Two changes were needed.

First, the encoder reads the window in both directions. Reading forwards only, the
summary of the window is the memory state after 120 steps, so anything that
happened at the start has to survive all 120 of them, and it does not.

Second, the decoder is given sines and cosines of its position in the window
instead of a single ramp from 0 to 1. A ramp tells the decoder where it is, but it
must then invent every oscillation of the ECG on its own. With a set of waves
supplied, it only has to combine them. This single change is what moved the model
off the flat line.

\subsection{Training and convergence} \label{subsec:training}

Both models are trained the same way: the Adam optimiser, batches of 256, mean
squared error as the loss, and a budget of at most 30 passes over the data for the
convolutional model and 40 for the recurrent one. Training stops
early when the validation error has not improved for five passes, and the weights
kept are those of the best pass, not the last one. Gradients are capped in size,
which an LSTM needs, since a single huge gradient can destroy the weights in one
step, and which costs the convolutional model nothing.

The two training curves could hardly be more different (\Cref{fig:aecurves}).

same model, trained on \emph{all} beats rather than normal ones only, collapses to
F1 \num{0.171}, below the random baseline. Shown abnormal beats during training,
the model learns to rebuild those too, and the reconstruction error stops
separating anything. Training on normal data alone is not a detail of the recipe,
it is the entire mechanism.

The recurrent model, despite all the work it took, finishes below Isolation
Forest at F1 \num{0.357}. But its AUC of \num{0.840} is the highest of the
label-free methods, marginally above the convolutional model's \num{0.839}. The
two numbers disagree because the errors of its normal beats are spread widely, as
the training curve already suggested. The extra context of a two-second window did
help the ranking. It did not survive the threshold.

share of abnormal beats are genuinely easy to rebuild, which

  \includegraphics[width=0.78\textwidth]{autoencoders/figures/cnn_signal_overlay.png}

The reason is visible in \Cref{fig:cnnrecon}. A ventricular beat is wide and
smooth. A smooth curve is exactly what convolutions and a squeezed representation
rebuild most easily, so an abnormal beat can be \emph{easier} to reconstruct than
a normal one, and the score points the wrong way. The method assumes that unusual
implies hard to rebuild, and on this signal that assumption is only partly true.

Two conclusions close the section. Deep learning did win among the label-free
methods, but by a margin that does not justify its cost here; a forest of random
cuts on hand-made features gets within \num{0.032} of it for a tiny fraction of
the computation. And the deepest model in this report still sits nearly
\num{0.26} of F1 below a Random Forest given the labels. The shortage in this
problem is supervision, not model capacity, which is the point
Section~\ref{sec:results} takes up.

A result that cannot be reproduced is an anecdote. This section describes what was
built so that every number in this report can be obtained again, by someone else,
on another machine, after the internship has ended.

The code is a proper installable Python package, not a folder of scripts. Twelve

The dependencies run one way only: \texttt{evaluation.py} and \texttt{splits.py}
know nothing about the models, while every model imports them. That is what makes
the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:results} trustworthy. There is exactly one
piece of code that computes an F1 score and exactly one that chooses a threshold,
so no method can be measured on slightly different terms from another.

The package installs with \texttt{pip install -e .} and declares its own
dependencies. PyTorch sits behind an optional \texttt{deep} extra, so anyone
interested only in the classical methods does not have to install it.

\subsection{Notebooks and reproducible workflow} \label{subsec:notebooks}

Six notebooks run the experiments, one per stage of the work: exploration,
statistical baselines, features and label-free models, supervised models, the
convolutional autoencoder, and the recurrent one.

The division of labour between notebook and package is strict. Every function
lives in the package; the notebooks call them, draw figures and save tables. No
notebook defines a model or a feature. This matters for two reasons: a function in
a notebook cannot be tested, and a notebook run out of order silently produces
results that depend on the order the cells happened to be executed in.

Each notebook writes its output into its own results folder, as a CSV table, a
Markdown table and figures. Every table in this report is copied from those files
rather than typed by hand, which removes a whole class of transcription error.

The data never enters the repository. A single function downloads the recordings
on demand, retrying a few times, since the PhysioNet server occasionally times
out. The \SI{650}{\mega\byte} of signals stay outside Git and outside the
container image.

\subsection{Containerisation} \label{subsec:container}

The image is built on a slim Python 3.11 base and holds the package, the tests and
the notebooks, with JupyterLab starting automatically. Four choices are worth
explaining.

PyTorch is installed from the CPU-only index. The default build pulls roughly
\SI{2.5}{\giga\byte} of GPU libraries that no machine used in this work could
exercise.

The container does not run as root. A user named \texttt{researcher} is created
and given ownership of the working directory, since without that ownership the
notebooks could not write a single figure.

The data and the results are mounted from outside rather than copied in. The
recordings would make the image unusable, and results written inside a container
disappear when it stops. Mounting them means the image holds only code, and the
same image works for anyone with their own copy of the data.

A compose file records the whole invocation, so starting the environment is one
command instead of a long line of options that has to be remembered correctly.

\subsection{Continuous integration} \label{subsec:ci}

Two automated workflows run on GitHub.

The first runs on every push and every pull request. It installs the package from
scratch, downloads a single record, and runs the test suite. Downloading one
record rather than all 48 is deliberate: it keeps the check under a minute while
still exercising the real reading and parsing code, rather than a mock that could
drift away from the real thing.

The second builds the container image and publishes it to the GitHub registry when
a version tag is pushed. A published image is a far stronger reproducibility
guarantee than a list of dependencies, since it fixes the exact versions that
produced the results.

The effect on the way the work was done is worth stating. Because the first
workflow runs on every pull request, no stage could be merged while the tests were
failing, and the checks guarding the DS1/DS2 split (Section~\ref{subsec:split})
therefore ran on every single change. A leak between training and test data could
not survive a single commit unnoticed.




\section{Verification} \label{sec:verification}

Validation, in Section~\ref{sec:validation}, asks whether the results say something
true about heartbeats. Verification asks the narrower question first: does the code
compute what this report claims it computes? A wrong number produced by correct
reasoning is still wrong, and the only defence is to check the machinery itself.

\subsection{Test strategy: unit tests, toy cases, invariants}
\label{subsec:teststrategy}

The package carries 64 tests spread over six files, one per module. They are not
regression tests: none of them checks that a function still returns the number it
returned yesterday. Each one states a property that must hold whatever the data,
and three kinds of property are used.

\paragraph{Shape and structure.} The cheapest and most frequently violated
promises. One row of features per beat, one score per window, no missing value in
the feature matrix, the meta columns dropped from the model input. These catch the
off-by-one errors that would otherwise silently misalign a whole record.

\paragraph{Mathematical identities.} Properties that follow from the definitions
and can be checked exactly. The five wavelet energy ratios must sum to one, since
the transform conserves energy. A perfectly regular rhythm must give a flat set of
rhythm features. A symmetric signal must have zero skewness, and Gaussian noise
near-zero excess kurtosis. A constant signal must give the right root mean square.
If any of these fails, the formula is wrong, not the data.

\paragraph{Behaviour on toy cases.} The most useful family. A small artificial
record is built with one beat per second, and selected beats are made deliberately
wider, as a ventricular beat is. Every detector is then required to give those
beats a higher score than the rest. Isolation Forest must score points far from the
crowd above points inside it; the one-class SVM the same; a Random Forest must
separate two clearly distinct classes; an autoencoder trained on narrow beats must
score wide ones higher. These tests do not check accuracy, they check the sign of
the answer, which is what a silent bug destroys first.

Two further points are worth stating. Every test that involves randomness is run
twice with the same seed and the outputs compared, so reproducibility is itself
tested rather than assumed. And the deep learning tests are skipped rather than
failed when PyTorch is absent, so the classical half of the package remains
testable in a minimal environment.

The evaluation module deserves a word of its own, since an error there would
corrupt the whole comparison while leaving each model apparently intact. The
threshold routine is checked against its own definition: cutting the scores at a
given percentile must leave the expected share of points above the line. The four
raw counts are stored beside every reported score, so precision and recall can be
recomputed by hand at any time. And the strongest check there is structural rather
than numerical: because the metrics live in a single module imported by every
notebook, a mistake in them would move all the methods in the same direction and
leave the ranking intact, and the ranking is what this report concludes from.

\subsection{Convergence of the training loss} \label{subsec:convergence}

For the two neural models, the training curve is itself a check: a model that has
not converged produces scores that mean nothing, whatever they look like.

Three guards are in place. Training stops when the validation error has not
improved for five passes, and the weights kept are those of the best pass rather
than the last. A test verifies that training actually lowers the loss on a simple
case. And a test requires the recurrent model to beat the flat-line answer, which
is the specific failure it exhibited before the changes of
Section~\ref{subsec:lstmae}: a mean squared error near \num{0.95} on a signal of
variance one means the model is predicting nothing at all.

\subsection{Reproducibility} \label{subsec:reproducibility}

Section~\ref{sec:software} described the machinery: the pinned environment, the
published container image, the data fetched from PhysioNet rather than shipped,
and every figure and table written to a results folder by the notebook that
produced it. One measure belongs here rather than there. Every source of randomness
takes a fixed seed, and two tests fit the same model twice with the same seed and
require identical scores, so reproducibility is checked rather than asserted. Every
notebook is also traceable to the files it wrote, which the repository documents.

Two gaps should be stated plainly. The container image digest is not recorded in
this report, so reproducibility is guaranteed at the level of a version tag rather
than an exact image. And no checksum of the downloaded recordings is stored, so a
silent change at the source would not be detected automatically. Both are recorded
in Section~\ref{subsec:debt} as technical debt.

\section{Validation} \label{sec:validation}

Section~\ref{sec:verification} established that the code computes what it claims.
This section asks the harder question: does what it computes say anything true
about heartbeats?

\subsection{Comparison with published results on the same protocol}
\label{subsec:published}

The comparison must be made carefully, because this work and the literature do not
answer the same question. Published systems assign each beat to one of the five
AAMI classes; the systems here answer normal or abnormal. A five-class score cannot
be set beside a two-class score directly, and pretending otherwise would be the
kind of comparison Section~\ref{subsec:sota} criticises.

Two things can nevertheless be checked.

\paragraph{The split is the right one.} The composition of DS2 reported in the
literature is \num{44258} normal beats, \num{1837} supraventricular, \num{3221}
ventricular, \num{388} fusion and \num{7} unclassifiable, giving an anomaly rate
of \num{0.1097}~\cite{dechazal2004}. The pipeline built here records exactly
\num{0.1097} on its own DS2. Reproducing an independently published figure to four
decimal places is strong evidence that the records, the paced-record exclusions and
the AAMI mapping have all been implemented as intended.

\paragraph{The difficulty is where the literature says it is.} Published results
under this protocol agree on one point across two decades. In the original work of
de Chazal et al.~\cite{dechazal2004}, ventricular beats were detected with a
sensitivity of \SI{67.3}{\percent} and supraventricular beats with only
\SI{53.3}{\percent}, while fusion and unclassifiable beats fared worse still. The
survey of Luz et al.~\cite{luz2016survey}, reviewing the methods published in the
decade that followed, reports the same ordering across the whole literature:
whatever the model, ventricular beats are recognised well and supraventricular
beats are not. Later work has narrowed the gap without closing it, and the
supraventricular class remains the weak point of every system evaluated under
this split.

That ordering is exactly what the analysis of Section~\ref{sec:supervised}
predicts from the features. Ventricular beats differ in both rhythm and shape;
supraventricular beats differ only in timing. A method that finds the second as
easily as the first would be suspicious rather than impressive.

Two comparisons are therefore avoided in this report. No score here is placed
beside a published five-class score. And no score here is placed beside a result
obtained without the inter-patient split, since those numbers are much higher and
measure a different thing~\cite{luz2016survey}.

\subsection{Clinical relevance of the errors} \label{subsec:clinical}

A single F1 hides the fact that the two kinds of error cost very different things.

A false alarm costs a physician a few seconds: the beat is displayed, glanced at
and dismissed. A missed beat costs the patient. The two are not symmetric, and F1
treats them as though they were. This is why Section~\ref{subsec:supresults}
declines to declare Random Forest better than XGBoost: the forest is more precise,
the boosting catches more, and which is preferable is a clinical judgement.

Whether a missed beat matters also depends on which beat is missed. A ventricular
beat carries more immediate clinical weight than a fusion beat, and a single
isolated abnormal beat matters less than a run of them. None of the methods here
distinguishes these cases, because all of them decide beat by beat with no memory
of what came before. A system reporting ``one odd beat in this minute'' and one
reporting ``forty odd beats in a row'' would receive identical treatment.

Two limits of the evaluation follow, and both make the reported figures optimistic
rather than pessimistic. The R-peak positions come from the annotations, so no
error made while finding the beats is counted. And the beats are judged
independently, so no credit or penalty attaches to catching a run of abnormal beats
rather than scattered ones.

\subsection{Domain of validity and limits} \label{subsec:validity}

The results of this report are valid under conditions that should be stated as
narrowly as they hold.

\paragraph{Where they hold.} On beat windows cut around R-peak positions that are
already known to be correct; on recordings from the MIT-BIH database, filtered and
normalised as in Section~\ref{subsec:preprocessing}; on adult patients without
pacemakers; on the first lead only; and with a threshold chosen once on DS1 and
applied unchanged to every patient in DS2.

\paragraph{Where they do not.} Nothing here has been tested on a patient with a
pacemaker, since those four records were excluded by design. Nothing has been
tested on a second lead, on modern recording equipment, or on any database other
than this one. No beat detector has been run, so a complete system would perform
worse than these numbers suggest. And no claim is made about which arrhythmia a
flagged beat represents: the output is a flag, not a diagnosis.

\paragraph{What the work does support.} Within those limits, three conclusions
survive, and they are stated in Section~\ref{subsec:contributions}. None of them
depends on the exact scores: all three depend on the ranking, and the ranking is
what the shared protocol of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} was built to protect.

\subsection{How much the numbers could move} \label{subsec:limits-numbers}

Every score in this report is a single value, and it is worth saying plainly how
firm those values are. The test set holds 22 patients. That is the real sample
size, not the \num{49692} beats, because beats from one patient resemble each
other far too closely to count as separate observations. With 22 patients, a
difference of a few points may say nothing more than which patients happened to
fall on which side of the split.

The per-class results make the same point from another direction. Ventricular
beats are caught by the supervised models at a recall near \num{0.95}, while
supraventricular beats sit near \num{0.16} and fusion beats near \num{0.28}. An
overall F1 is therefore dominated by the V class, and a method that scored slightly
better overall might simply have met a test group with more V beats in it.

Two consequences follow, and they are applied throughout this report. Large gaps
are treated as real: the \num{0.294} of F1 that supervision adds over the same
features, and the collapse measured in \Cref{tab:ablations} when the autoencoder is
trained on all beats rather than normal ones only, are far too big to come from
patient sampling. Small gaps are not: the \num{0.032} separating the convolutional
autoencoder from Isolation Forest is within the range that 22 patients could
produce by chance, so the fair statement is that those two methods perform
comparably, and the neural one costs far more computation to do so.

This is why Section~\ref{subsec:deliverables} set the objective as a trustworthy
ranking rather than a target score. The ranking holds where the gaps are wide, and
this report claims nothing where they are narrow.

The previous sections reported each family of methods on its own terms. This one
puts them side by side and asks what the comparison actually taught.

autoencoder reaches \num{0.357} in \SI{17.2}{\minute}. Cost and accuracy do not
move together in this problem, which is the main practical lesson of the
internship.

\subsection{What supervision actually buys} \label{subsec:supervision-buys}

The clean experiment of this internship is the pair Isolation Forest and Random
Forest. They read the same 35 numbers, on the same beats, under the same split. The
only difference is that one is told the answers during training.

That difference is worth \num{0.294} of F1, from \num{0.427} to \num{0.721}. For
comparison, replacing one hand-picked number with 35 engineered features bought
\num{0.054}, and reading the raw signal with a neural network bought \num{0.032}
more. Supervision is worth roughly five times what all the feature work is worth
put together.

But the gain is lopsided, and the shape of it is the more interesting result.
Precision rises from \num{0.343} to \num{0.837}, while recall rises only from
\num{0.566} to \num{0.633}. Labels do not teach the model to see more; they teach
it to speak less often and be right when it does. An anomaly detector flags
everything unusual, and most unusual beats are not abnormal ones. Told what an
abnormal beat looks like, the model stops flagging the merely odd.

This has a direct practical reading. If the goal is to reduce the physician's
workload, labels are indispensable. If the goal is to miss as few abnormal beats as
possible, they buy much less, and a label-free method costing seconds is not far
behind.

\subsection{The S-class ceiling} \label{subsec:sclass}

The overall figures conceal a reversal that only per-class numbers reveal
(\Cref{tab:perclass}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \small
  \caption{Recall per AAMI class on DS2. Q is omitted: only 7 such beats survive
  the exclusion of the paced records, too few to mean anything.}
  \label{tab:perclass}

  \end{tabular}

The V column explains the overall ranking: ventricular beats are the largest
abnormal class, the supervised models catch \SI{95}{\percent} of them, and that
alone carries most of the F1 gap. Nothing surprising there.

The S column is the surprise. The best supervised model catches \num{0.161} of the
supraventricular beats. The convolutional autoencoder, which was never shown a
single label, catches \num{0.654} of them: four times as many.

The explanation is that the two families are trained to do different things. A
supervised model minimises its error over a set in which nine beats in ten are
normal and supraventricular beats are one in forty. Ignoring the S class costs it
almost nothing in total error, so it learns to ignore it. That is not a flaw in the
model; it is the model doing exactly what it was asked. An autoencoder is never
asked to be accurate overall. It is asked only what a normal beat looks like, and
anything that does not fit is flagged. A supraventricular beat, arriving early and
disturbing the rhythm, does not fit.

Two conclusions follow. First, that the ``S-class ceiling'' reported across the
literature (Section~\ref{subsec:published}) is at least partly a consequence of
optimising a single overall score under heavy imbalance, not only a consequence of
S beats looking normal. Second, and more usefully, that the two families fail on
different beats. The two autoencoders alone disagree on thousands of beats, and
taking either flag rather than both raises recall to \num{0.726}, above what either
achieves alone. Combining a supervised model for precision with a label-free one
for coverage of the rare classes is the most promising direction this work points
to, and Section~\ref{subsec:perspectives} returns to it.

The F column should not be passed over. Every method is poor on fusion beats, and
the autoencoders are worst of all, at \num{0.064} and \num{0.018}. A fusion beat is
by definition a blend of a normal and a ventricular beat, so it sits halfway
between what the model learned and what it did not. Reconstruction error, which
measures distance from normal, is close to blind to it.



\section{Conclusion} \label{sec:conclusion}

\subsection{Contributions} \label{subsec:contributions}

This internship set out to answer one question: can a machine find abnormal
heartbeats on its own, and what does it cost to help it? Four contributions answer
it.

The first is a complete and reproducible chain from a raw half-hour recording to a
decision on every single beat, built as an installable package rather than a set of
scripts, covered by 64 property-based tests, checked automatically on every change,
and shipped as a container image. The software is a result of this work, not
scaffolding for it: without it, none of the numbers below could be produced again.

The second is a fixed measuring protocol, decided before any model was written and
reused unchanged by every method. Records are split by patient, the threshold is
chosen only on the training half, and one module computes every score. This is what
makes the comparison meaningful, and it was the single most consequential decision
of the internship. The composition of the test set reproduces the published figure
exactly (Section~\ref{subsec:published}), which confirms the split was implemented
as intended.

The third is the comparison itself, spanning three statistical baselines, two
label-free models on engineered features, two supervised models and two neural
networks, all measured identically. Its central finding is that supervision is
worth about five times what all the feature engineering is worth put together, and
that what it buys is precision rather than coverage: the model learns to stop
flagging beats that are merely unusual, not to see beats it was previously missing.

The fourth is the per-class analysis, and it is the finding that would not have
appeared without the discipline of the first three. Supraventricular beats, the
class the literature has struggled with for twenty years, are caught four times
better by an autoencoder that never saw a label than by the best supervised model.
The reason is not that the autoencoder is stronger, but that a model minimising
overall error under a nine-to-one imbalance learns that ignoring a rare class costs
it almost nothing. The two families of method therefore fail on different beats,
which is a more useful result than either score alone.

\subsection{Limitations and technical debt} \label{subsec:debt}

Four limits concern what the results mean.

The beats are given, not found. Every score in this report assumes the R-peak
positions are already correct, because they come from the annotations. A complete
system would have to find them and would perform worse than reported here.

One database, one lead, one patient group. Forty-eight recordings from the 1970s,
first lead only, no pacemaker patients, and nothing tested on any other data.

Fusion beats defeat every method, and the autoencoders most of all. A beat that
sits halfway between normal and abnormal is nearly invisible to a score that
measures distance from normal.

The recurrent autoencoder memorises its training patients. Its validation error
settles at four times its training error, which is why its ranking is good and its
thresholded decisions are not.

Four further items are technical debt in the software, recorded here so they are
not rediscovered later.

The container image digest is not stored, so reproducibility is guaranteed at the
level of a version tag rather than an exact image. No checksum of the downloaded
recordings is kept, so a silent change at the source would pass unnoticed. Per-beat
scores are saved only for the two neural models, which is why the uncertainty of
the classical methods could not be quantified without rerunning them. And the
experiments still live in notebooks rather than in a single pipeline module, so
running the whole benchmark end to end means executing six notebooks in order.

\subsection{Personal assessment} \label{subsec:personal}

Three things about the way this internship was run are worth recording, since
they cost time and changed how I work.

The first is that the protocol had to come before the models. My instinct at the
start was to fit something and see what happened, and it took the reading of
Section~\ref{subsec:sota} to understand that a score obtained without an
inter-patient split simply does not answer the question. Writing the split, the
metrics and the threshold rule first felt like a delay of several days; in the end
it is the only reason the ten methods can be put in one table at all.

The second is how much of the useful information came from things that did not
work. The one-second Z-score scoring below chance, the one-class SVM losing to a
baseline, the recurrent autoencoder answering a flat line for a whole training
run: each of those took longer to diagnose than the successful runs took to
produce, and each of them is reported here because the diagnosis is the result.
Learning to check a model against a trivial baseline before believing its score
is the habit I take away from this work.

The third is the working method. One issue per task, one pull request per stage
and tests running on every push were imposed at the start, and I understood their
value only in the middle of the internship, when the checks guarding the DS1/DS2
split caught a change that would otherwise have leaked training patients into the
test set silently. Organising the work in stages that each end with something
merged also made it possible to say, at any point, what was finished and what was
not.

What I would do differently is straightforward. I would save the per-beat scores
of every method from the beginning rather than only for the neural models, since
their absence is what prevented any quantitative statement about uncertainty
(Section~\ref{subsec:limits-numbers}), and I would move the experiments out of the
notebooks into a single pipeline module earlier, rather than leaving it as the
technical debt described above.

\subsection{Perspectives} \label{subsec:perspectives}

The results point to one direction more strongly than any other, and to four
smaller ones.

\paragraph{Combine the two families.} This is the clearest lead. The supervised
models are precise and blind to the rare classes; the label-free models are noisy
and catch what the others miss. They fail on different beats, and taking the flag
of either raises coverage above what either reaches alone. A system using a
supervised model for its decisions and an anomaly detector to catch what that model
was trained to ignore would address the S-class ceiling directly, rather than
hoping a larger model will eventually close it.

\paragraph{Close the pipeline.} Adding a beat detector, of the Pan and Tompkins
family, would remove the strongest optimistic bias in this report and give figures
a complete system could actually deliver.

\paragraph{Test elsewhere.} Running the same protocol unchanged on a second
annotated database would separate what the methods learned about heartbeats from
what they learned about this particular collection of recordings.

\paragraph{Choose the threshold for the task.} The gap between AUC and F1 found
throughout this report says a single threshold serving 22 patients loses much of
what the ranking knows. Adapting the threshold per patient, or setting it from the
clinical cost of a missed beat rather than from F1, is cheap to try and would likely
gain more than a larger model.

\paragraph{Put numbers on the uncertainty.} With 22 test patients, small differences
are not measurable, and this report states which of its claims that affects
(Section~\ref{subsec:limits-numbers}). Resampling whole records to attach an
interval to each score would make that judgement quantitative instead of
argued.

\paragraph{Carry it to vibration signals.} The internship was framed as defect
detection, and the pipeline transfers as it stands: filter, cut into events,
describe each event with numbers, score, threshold, and measure with a split that
keeps machines apart, the inter-machine counterpart of the inter-patient split
used here. The descriptors transfer with modification, spectral and wavelet
features being if anything better suited to rotating machinery, where faults
announce themselves as characteristic frequencies. Two things do not. The R-peak
has no equivalent, so segmentation becomes a real and unsolved step rather than a
free one; and a monitored machine is healthy almost all of the time, so anomalies
would be far rarer than one beat in nine, which pushes further still toward the
label-free half of this comparison.

Beyond the cardiology, the transferable outcome is the method rather than the
model: describe the signal, fix the protocol before fitting anything, compare
everything on identical terms, and read the per-class results before believing the
average. That discipline applies unchanged to a vibration signal from a bearing,
where labels are far scarcer than they are here and the label-free half of this
comparison would carry most of the weight.
